1.0 Facility Process Description:  
The facility will generate electricity using fuel cells. Fuel cells emit NOx, CO, VOC, SO2, PM, PM10, 
and PM2.5 emissions as a byproduct of chemical and electrical (chemoelectrical) reactions. Other 
byproducts from power generation include heat and steam.   
 
The facility will use a Bloom Energy Server System, which is a type of solid oxide fuel cell system 
(SOFC). The SOFC system converts natural gas into electricity through an electrochemical 
reaction. Each individual fuel cell is composed of three layers: a solid ceramic electrolyte 
sandwiched between a cathode and an anode, where the anode and cathode are made from 
inks that coat the electrolyte. No precious metals, corrosive acids, or molten materials are 
required. Natural gas enters the anode side, where it is mixed with steam to produce reformed 
fuel through Direct Internal Reforming (DIR). As the reformed fuel crosses the anode, it attracts 
oxygen ions from the air on the cathode side. The oxygen ions combine with the reformed fuel 
to produce electricity, water, heat, and carbon dioxide. The water and heat are recycled to 
produce the steam needed to reform the fuel, enabling a highly efficient electrochemical 
reaction occurring at 700–900 degrees Celsius.  
 
Individual cells are combined into stacks, then into power modules containing hundreds of 
individual fuel cells that produce direct current power. The modules are packaged into Energy 
Servers, which combine the power modules with a fuel processing module and an AC module 
containing DC-to-AC converters and transformers to produce 325 kW of net AC power. A 
minority of smaller units produce 260 kW of net AC power.

The fuel cells are organized/arranged as follows: cell and stack -> power module -> energy server 
-> cluster -> inverter group/platform. The proposed Bloom Energy Server System deploys co-
functioning Energy Servers to create an integrated system capable of sustaining the target 
output represented for each applicable scenario (260 kW or 325 kW). Depleted anode exhaust, 
which is approximately 95 percent carbon dioxide once dried, is discharged to the atmosphere 
as greenhouse gas (GHG). The air emissions from the Energy Servers consist of depleted anode 
exhaust, which contains criteria air pollutants consisting of NOx, CO, VOC, SO2, PM, PM10, PM2.5, 
and HAPs. The applicant seeks authorization to operate the Bloom Energy Server System at its 
maximum rated load for 8,760 hours per year. 
 
Startup, Shutdown, and Maintenance (SSM) routine or predictable emissions: Maintenance 
activities include hot swap of power modules approximately once per five years, replacement of 
desulfurization bed media once per two years, and as-needed individual component (e.g., 
blower) replacements. Air emissions (natural gas venting) will occur during desulfurization bed 
media replacements and during desulfurization unit initial start-ups. Approximately ninety-five 
Central Desulfurization System (CDS) skids/blocks will be installed, consisting of two vessels in a 
lead/lag configuration for desulfurization. Each vessel has a volume of 1,370 standard cubic feet.  
 
2.0 Description of this Modification:  
This permit application is for a new source review (NSR) construction permit submitted pursuant 
to 20.2.72.200.A(1) NMAC for a microgrid power generation facility to generate power for a 
nearby (potentially adjacent) data center. The facility will generate power using fuel cells which 
are solid oxide fuel cell (SOCF) Bloom Energy Server System 260 kW and 325 kW. Energy Server 
“clusters” are routed to a total of 2,275 exhaust stacks as emission sources. The site will be a 
major source for Title V (20.2.70 NMAC).
